Case description

For WPL2 I worked on a multi-week case at a partner organisation. The brief was to design and build a digital component that would meaningfully improve an internal workflow. The first weeks were spent observing the team, mapping the current process and identifying the most painful steps. From this research we framed a focused problem statement and aligned with stakeholders before any design work started.

The case had real constraints: a fixed deadline, existing technical stack, accessibility requirements and end-users with varied technical comfort. These constraints shaped most of the decisions and forced trade-offs between scope, polish and feasibility. The eventual scope landed on a small but complete feature that could be shipped, tested with real users, and iterated on within the available timebox.

My contribution

I owned the design and front-end implementation of the feature, while collaborating closely with a back-end developer and a UX mentor. Concretely, I ran two short user-research sessions, translated the findings into a flow diagram, produced a low-fidelity prototype in Figma, and iterated based on feedback from the team. After alignment I moved into implementation using the organisation's existing component library, adding only what was missing.

Beyond execution I tried to be a useful team member: writing clear pull-request descriptions, asking for review early, documenting decisions in the project wiki and demoing progress in the weekly stand-up. When blockers appeared I raised them quickly rather than silently working around them, which kept the timeline predictable.

What I learned

  • Scoping ruthlessly is more valuable than building fast — a small thing that ships beats a large thing that doesn't.
  • Accessibility decisions made early are nearly free; retrofitted later they are expensive.
  • Writing things down (decisions, trade-offs, open questions) saves the team hours later.
  • Asking for feedback at 60% is more useful than polishing to 95% in isolation.
  • Real users behave differently from imagined ones — testing with two real people beats five assumptions.
